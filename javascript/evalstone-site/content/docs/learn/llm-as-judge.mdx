Using a model to grade a model is the technique that made evals practical
for open-ended outputs. It is also the technique with the most ways to fool
yourself. This chapter is the checklist.

## Write a rubric, not a vibe

"Rate the helpfulness 1–10" produces noise. A rubric names the criteria,
defines each level, and tells the judge what to ignore:

```text
You are grading a customer-support answer.

Criteria:
1. Correctness — every factual claim is supported by the POLICY text below.
2. Completeness — the answer addresses every part of the QUESTION.
3. Tone — polite, does not blame the customer.

Score 1–5:
5 = all three criteria fully met
4 = minor wording issue, no factual or completeness problem
3 = one part of a multi-part question missed, facts correct
2 = a factual claim not supported by the policy
1 = wrong, off-topic, or rude

Ignore: formatting, length, whether it uses markdown.
Output JSON: {"score": <1-5>, "reason": "<one sentence>"}
```

Specific criteria, a small scale with defined levels, an explicit ignore
list, structured output. Every one of those is there because its absence
produced a bad judge somewhere.

## Use small scales

Models are bad at 1–10 and worse at 1–100. 1–5 with level definitions, or
pass/fail with a reason, is where agreement with humans is highest. Map the
scale to 0..1 for aggregation and keep the raw value in the reason.

## Know the biases

- **Position bias.** In pairwise comparison the judge prefers the first (or
  sometimes the second) answer. Always run both orders, or randomize.
- **Verbosity bias.** Longer answers score higher. Put "ignore length" in the
  rubric and check whether the correlation survives.
- **Self-preference.** A model rates its own outputs higher. Use a different
  model family for the judge when you can.
- **Anchoring on confidence.** A confident wrong answer scores higher than a
  hedged right one. Correctness criteria must say "supported by the source",
  not "sounds right".
- **Reference leakage.** If the reference answer is in the judge prompt, the
  judge grades similarity to the reference, not quality. Only include it
  when similarity *is* the criterion.

## Calibrate before you trust

A judge is a measurement instrument; you check an instrument against a
standard. The standard is **human labels**:

1. Have people grade 100+ samples with the same rubric.
2. Run the judge on the same samples.
3. Compute agreement: exact-match rate, and Cohen's kappa (which corrects
   for agreement by chance). Look at the confusion matrix, not just the
   number: a judge that is always one level generous is fixable; one that is
   random in the middle of the scale is not.
4. Iterate on the rubric (not on the samples) until agreement is acceptable.
   For most product work, kappa above 0.6 with no systematic bias is usable;
   above 0.8 is excellent.
5. **Freeze the judge version.** The rubric text, the model, the few-shot
   examples. A changed judge is a changed ruler; runs across the change are
   not comparable.

Repeat when the judge model is upgraded.

## Control the cost

- **Temperature 0** and a **cache** keyed on (judge version, input, output).
  Re-running a suite where 90% of outputs did not change costs 10%.
- **Deterministic checks first.** An output that fails the JSON schema does
  not need a $0.002 judgement of its tone.
- **Small judge models** are fine for narrow rubrics once calibrated. Use
  the expensive model to label, the cheap one to judge, and let calibration
  tell you if the cheap one is good enough.
- **Report cost per run** so a judge that quietly started re-running shows
  up as a number.

## A judge error is not a pass

Timeouts, malformed JSON, refusals: the judge did not score the sample. That
sample is an **error**, counted separately, and the gate should fail if the
error rate exceeds a small tolerance. A suite that treats errors as passes
goes green the day the judge API goes down.
